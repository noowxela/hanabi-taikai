# The "Logic" of Pixel Art: A Primer for Developers About to Build a Pixel Art Game

> Written for Alex. The goal isn't to turn you into a pixel artist. It's to help you understand, before you write your first line of engine code, why pixel art "follows rules" and which of those rules directly affect your project settings.
> Research verified: 2026-10-06 (UTC+8). Every linked claim in this document comes from a page actually opened during this research. Content marked "[common practice, no citable source found]" is industry common knowledge; use your own judgment.

---

## 0. Pixel Art in One Sentence

The core of pixel art isn't "low resolution". It's that **every pixel is deliberately controlled**. Slynyrd considers "intention" the "number one defining factor" of pixel art: pixels should be "placed with deliberate thought in a precise manner" ([Pixelblog 5 – Back to Basics](https://www.slynyrd.com/blog/2018/5/16/pixelblog-5-back-to-basics), 2018-05-22). Lospec's beginner article says the same thing: it's about having control over every pixel on your canvas, and "none of the pixels are generated by a computer" ([Pixel Art: Where to Start](https://lospec.com/articles/pixel-art-where-to-start/), 2019-06-14).

For a developer, this means: **the engine must never "alter" your pixels**, whether through interpolation blur, non-integer scaling, or rotation resampling. Every section below really revolves around this one idea.

---

## 1. Resolution and Pixel Density

### 1.1 Trade-offs Between 8 / 16 / 32 / 64

- **The lower the resolution, the more you have to choose which details to keep.** Saint11's Resizing tutorial says that as resolution decreases, you need to start choosing which details are important; higher resolutions usually need more colors; and bigger resolutions allow more realistic styles and rounder shapes ([Saint11 – Resizing](https://saint11.art/img/pixel-tutorials/Resizing.gif), see the [tutorial index](https://saint11.art/blog/pixel-art-tutorials/), 2020-07-12).
- **Tile size**: Slynyrd says 16×16 px is "probably the most common size" and recommends powers of 2 because their multiples are the most consistent. He also says that for tiles, "anything over 32x32px seems like overkill for pixel art" ([Pixelblog 28 – Side View Tiles](https://www.slynyrd.com/blog/2020/5/21/pixelblog-28-side-view-tiles), 2020-05-26). He also has an entry built around an 8×8 tilemap with the NES palette ([Pixelblog catalogue #59](https://www.slynyrd.com/pixelblog-catalogue)).
- Practical conversion (simple arithmetic): at 320×180, a 16 px tall character is about 1/11 of the screen height; at 640×360, about 1/22. The smaller the character, the more "open" the scene feels, but the fewer readable details (faces, weapons) you get.
- [common practice, no citable source found] Rough rule of thumb: 8 px suits minimalist or Game Boy/PICO-8 styles; 16 px is the mainstream indie choice; 32 px leaves room for facial expressions and clothing; 64 px multiplies the workload (animation frame count × area).

### 1.2 Consistent Pixel Scale and Mixels

**One "art pixel" must be the same size on screen across the entire frame.** If you scale a 16 px prop up 2× and place it next to a 1× character, you get **mixels** (mixed pixels). Wiktionary defines it as, in pixel art, "a pixel of incoherent or inconsistent size when compared to the rest of the artwork as a whole" ([mixel – Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/mixel), last edited 2026-01-24). The fix isn't scaling, it's redrawing: Saint11 opens with "scaling and rotating pixel art should always be avoided; to do it properly there's no easy escape, you need to redraw it" ([Resizing](https://saint11.art/img/pixel-tutorials/Resizing.gif)).

### 1.3 Why Rotation and Non-Integer Scaling Break the Grid

Screens can only display whole pixels. The Godot docs give a concrete example: a 640×360 viewport in a 1366×768 window gives a scale factor of about 2.133×, so each art pixel maps to 2.133×2.133 screen pixels. In practice that means "some become 2 pixels, some become 3", so line widths vary and checkerboards look uneven. Switching to integer scaling (2×, i.e. 1280×720 with black bars) makes it even ([Godot – Multiple resolutions](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/rendering/multiple_resolutions.html), Godot 4.7 docs, date not shown). Rotation has the same problem: after a 45° rotation every point lands between grid cells, so resampling duplicates or drops pixels, and outlines and single-pixel lines break. Aseprite includes the RotSprite rotation algorithm to mitigate this (see the [Steam page feature list](https://store.steampowered.com/app/431730/Aseprite/)), but Saint11's advice is still to redraw whenever you can.

## 2. Lines

- **Perfect lines**: lines with slopes of 0:1, 1:2, 1:1, 2:1, and 1:0 are the easiest for the eye to follow and are called "perfect lines". "Intermediate" lines that alternate segments of 2 and 1 to simulate 1.5 look much less appealing and should be used sparingly ([OpenGameArt – Chapter 2: Lines and Curves](https://opengameart.org/content/chapter-2-lines-and-curves), date not shown).
- **Equal-length segments**: a straight diagonal must be made of **small segments of identical length**, e.g. 2-2-2-2. If even one segment differs, the eye immediately notices a "bump". That's a jaggy ([Lospec – Pixel Art Outlines Tutorial](https://lospec.com/articles/pixel-art-outlines/), 2016-04-03).
- **Jaggies: definition and fix**: the Pixel Joint tutorial says jaggies occur "when a pixel or group of pixels are out of place, interrupting the flow of a line". The usual fix is to **make segment lengths uniform**, adding AA if needed ([Pixel Joint – The Pixel Art Tutorial](https://pixeljoint.com/forum/forum_posts.asp?TID=11299&PD=0), posts from 2010-11 to 2014-07).
- **Curves**: segment lengths should **change monotonically**. Lospec's counter-example: 5,2,1,2,1 produces a lump, while 5,2,2,1,1 is smooth. On a circle, the longest segments go at the top, bottom, left, and right, getting shorter toward the 45° directions ([Pixel Art Outlines Tutorial](https://lospec.com/articles/pixel-art-outlines/)).
- **Doubles (L-shaped corners)**: the segments of a single-pixel line should **only connect diagonally**. Otherwise you get L-shaped corners that locally thicken the line and make it look "fat and sketchy" (same Lospec article). Aseprite's "pixel perfect strokes" is a brush option that automatically removes these extra pixels ([Steam feature list](https://store.steampowered.com/app/431730/Aseprite/)).
  - Note: **doubles are not "orphan pixels"**. An orphan pixel is a lone pixel of noise with no same-colored neighbors around it; that's a different problem. [the terminology distinction is common usage]

Each page offers downloads in `.hex`, `.gpl`, `.pal`, `.ase`, and other formats that import directly into Aseprite. **Why limit colors**: fewer colors keep the art style consistent and force you to describe form through value. Slynyrd warns in his tile tutorial that with "too many colors... the texture will become blurry" ([Pixelblog 20 – Top Down Tiles](https://www.slynyrd.com/blog/2019/8/27/pixelblog-20-top-down-tiles), 2019-08-27).

### 3.2 Color Ramps and Hue Shifting

Slynyrd's method ([Pixelblog 1 – Color Palettes](https://www.slynyrd.com/blog/2018/1/10/pixelblog-1-color-palettes), 2018-01-16):

- A **color ramp** is a set of colors that work well together, arranged by brightness.
- As brightness increases, **decrease saturation**, or the colors become eye-searing; very dark colors with high saturation look "heavy". Generally, darker colors have more saturation, and saturation often peaks in the middle swatch (he notes this isn't a hard rule).
- **Hue shifting**: shifting hue along the ramp. His example palette uses 9 swatches per ramp with a positive 20° hue shift between adjacent swatches, and he says "20 is about as high as I go". "Straight ramps" that only change brightness and saturation lack interest and are hard to harmonize with other ramps.

### 3.3 Value Contrast

Whether a form reads clearly depends mainly on value differences, not hue differences. Lospec's outline article stresses that the point of an outline is to **increase contrast, never decrease it** ([Outlines Part 2: Using Color](https://lospec.com/articles/pixel-art-outlines-part-2-using-color/), 2016-04-03). [common practice, no citable source found] Self-check: temporarily convert the frame to grayscale; if the player character and background are still instantly distinguishable, you pass.

## 4. Shading and Form

- **Set the light source first**: Slynyrd says a light source should be established at the very start of any illustration; it dictates the flow of shadows and where highlights go ([Pixelblog 6 – Light and Shadow](https://www.slynyrd.com/blog/2018/6/15/pixelblog-6-light-and-shadow), 2018-06-19).
- **Pillow shading**: darkening in rings outward from the center. The problem "is not because the light source is frontal"; it's that the shading follows the flat outline instead of showing how three-dimensional forms are lit ([Pixel Joint tutorial](https://pixeljoint.com/forum/forum_posts.asp?TID=11299&PD=0)).
- **Banding**: when neighboring bands of pixels line up at the same x or y coordinate, the grid is exposed and the apparent resolution drops. Common forms include hugging (an outline tightly following a color field), fat pixels, skip-one banding, and 45° banding (same source).
- **When to use dithering**: the most common mistake is using too much. If dithering covers half your sprite, you'd be better off adding a color. Dithering is best used to **taper the edges and ends of a color field** as a transition between two colors; otherwise it turns into unwanted texture (same source). [common practice] Dithering tends to flicker in animation and on scrolling backgrounds, so test it in-engine before using it over large areas.
- **Manual anti-aliasing (manual AA)**: only horizontal, vertical, and 45° lines avoid jagged steps; every other angle produces stair-steps. Manual AA means **hand-placing intermediate colors** at the stair-step corners to ease the transition (same source). Avoid "external AA": game sprites appear on changing backgrounds, so AA toward one background color on the outer edge will look wrong on a different background.
- **Outlines**: outlines separate objects from the background and from other objects, which is why they're common in game sprites ([Lospec – Outlines Tutorial](https://lospec.com/articles/pixel-art-outlines/)).
- **Selout (selective outlining): the correct definition**:
  - Lospec's version: **use differently colored outlines in different places**. Where the object is darkest, use a dark outline; where it's lightest, use a lighter one. The goal is to keep the contrast between outline and object consistent all the way around, and you also have to take the background color into account ([Outlines Part 2](https://lospec.com/articles/pixel-art-outlines-part-2-using-color/)).
  - The Pixel Joint tutorial is stricter: sel-out (also called broken outlines) is "anti-aliasing an outline to a background color", and it explicitly says it is **not the same as shading an outline according to a light source**. It works only for predictable scenarios, such as a game where the background is consistently dark ([Pixel Joint tutorial](https://pixeljoint.com/forum/forum_posts.asp?TID=11299&PD=0)).
  - What both definitions share: **selout means the outline color varies by position instead of being a solid black line**. It has nothing to do with "orphan pixels".

## 5. Animation

- **Walking**: Saint11's Walk Cycle gives two options: **12 frames** (6 per leg, fluid) and **6 frames** (3 per leg, useful at low resolution). The 6 important frames, in order, are Contact, Down, Down+ (lowest point), Prepare Passing, Passing, and Up (highest point); arms always move with the opposite leg ([Saint11 – Walk](https://saint11.art/img/pixel-tutorials/Walk.gif)).
- **Running**: Saint11's Simple Run compares **8 frames and 16 frames**. A simple cycle can be understood as 4 frames, then repeated with the other leg ([Saint11 – RunCycleSimple](https://saint11.art/img/pixel-tutorials/RunCycleSimple.gif)).
- **Timing (numbers with a source)**: in Slynyrd's example, each pose in the 8-frame run cycle is shown for **80 ms**, and each pose in the 4-frame cycle for **160 ms**. Both total 640 ms; when you remove frames, you must also adjust playback speed to preserve rhythm and energy. He also says "too many in-between frames can make your motion look sluggish". Mega Man's run uses only 3 drawings (a 4-frame loop with the passing frame used twice) ([Pixelblog 8 – Intro to Animation](https://www.slynyrd.com/blog/2018/8/19/pixelblog-8-intro-to-animation), 2018-08-21).
- **Idle**: 8-bit JRPGs often reuse a 2-frame walk for the idle; the most common type is the "bouncy breathing" idle. A small team may not be able to give every character an 8-frame idle; the key is consistency (same source).
- **Sub-pixel animation**: Saint11 says you can't move something less than 1 pixel, but you can "fake" it: fade colors to create an illusion of <1 px movement, move the interior before or after the silhouette, mostly move light rather than shapes so the silhouette barely changes; too many colors make everything look blurry ([Saint11 – Subpixel](https://saint11.art/img/pixel-tutorials/Subpixel.gif)).
- **Smear frames / motion blur**: Saint11's Motion Blur notes that motion blur "smears" the image, as if trying to connect it to the previous frame, and that it's a cheap way to convey complicated fast movement; lighter colors "bleed" over darker ones, and darker colors are used for fading ([Saint11 – MotionBlur](https://saint11.art/img/pixel-tutorials/MotionBlur.gif)).
- **Squash & stretch and anticipation**: the most important rule is "**mass never changes**": when an object gets wider, it must get shorter. Stretching is a cheap way to smooth fast animations; **anticipation** squashes the object in the **opposite direction** of the action first; stiff objects should deform less; and you can add squash-and-stretch tweens to objects in your game code ([Saint11 – Squash](https://saint11.art/img/pixel-tutorials/Squash.gif)).

## 6. Tiles

- **Size**: 16×16 is the most common; use powers of 2 (see the Slynyrd source in §1.1).
- **Seamless**: let clusters overlap the edges and loop around to the other side to hide seams; don't let any area of the tile dominate visually, or the repetition becomes obvious; keep colors few ([Pixelblog 20](https://www.slynyrd.com/blog/2019/8/27/pixelblog-20-top-down-tiles)). In Aseprite, turn on `View > Tiled Mode` to preview tiling live as you draw (same source).
- **Autotiling**:
  - **Tiled**: there are three kinds of Terrain Set. Corner Set (matches at corners; a complete set with 2 terrains has 16 tiles); Edge Set (matches at sides, good for roads, fences, and platforms; also 16 tiles with 2 terrains); Mixed Set (matches both corners and sides; a complete set with 2 terrains has 256 tiles, but reduced sets like the **47-tile Blob tileset** also work). A Terrain Set can contain up to 254 terrains ([Tiled – Using Terrains](https://doc.mapeditor.org/en/stable/manual/terrain/), date not shown).
  - **16 tiles / marching squares**: [common practice] The yes/no combinations of four corners give exactly 2⁴=16 cases. That's the 16 tiles of a Tiled Corner Set, often called marching-squares-style tiling.
  - **Godot 4 terrain sets**: create a Terrain Set in the TileSet. The modes are `Match Corners and Sides`, `Match Corners`, and `Match Sides`. The docs say these modes correspond to Godot 3.x's 2×2, 3×3, and 3×3 minimal bitmask modes (the docs don't pair them one-to-one) and are similar to Tiled. Then set Terrain Peering Bits on each tile; IDs start at 0, and -1 means empty ([Godot – Using TileSets](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/2d/using_tilesets.html), Godot 4.7 docs, date not shown). [common practice] In the community, "3×3 minimal" usually refers to the 47-tile blob set.

## 7. Engine Setup

### 7.1 Base Resolution and Integer Scaling (Arithmetic)

| Base resolution | → 1280×720 | → 1920×1080 | → 2560×1440 | → 3840×2160 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 320×180 | ×4 ✅ | ×6 ✅ | ×8 ✅ | ×12 ✅ |
| 384×216 | 3.33 → ×3 = 1152×648 + black bars | **×5 ✅** (384×5=1920, 216×5=1080) | 6.67 → ×6 = 2304×1296 + black bars | ×10 ✅ |
| 480×270 | 2.67 → ×2 = 960×540 + black bars | **×4 ✅** | 5.33 → ×5 = 2400×1350 + black bars | ×8 ✅ |
| 640×360 | ×2 ✅ | **×3 ✅** | ×4 ✅ | ×6 ✅ |

All of these base resolutions are integer multiples of 1080p. 320×180 and 640×360 have no black bars on any of the four common 16:9 screens. Godot's official docs also say most pixel art games use viewport sizes between 256×224 and 640×480, and recommend 640×360 as a baseline because it scales to 720p/1080p/1440p/4K "without any black bars" with integer scaling ([Multiple resolutions](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/rendering/multiple_resolutions.html)).

### 7.2 Recommended Godot 4 Settings (checked against the official docs)

- `Display > Window > Size`: set to your base resolution, e.g. 640×360.
- `Display > Window > Stretch > Mode` = `viewport` (renders at the base resolution first, then scales up as a whole; if you want to allow sub-pixel movement or rotation, use `canvas_items` instead).
- `Stretch > Aspect` = `keep` (black bars) or `expand`.
- `Stretch > Scale Mode` = `integer` (available since Godot 4.2; rounds the scale factor down to an integer, "useful to prevent distortion when displaying pixel art").
- `Rendering > Textures > Canvas Textures > Default Texture Filter`: change to `Nearest` (the docs list this setting's default as 1; `TEXTURE_FILTER_NEAREST` reads only the nearest pixel, so the image stays "pixelated"). Sources: [ProjectSettings](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_projectsettings.html), [CanvasItem](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_canvasitem.html).
- `Rendering > 2D > Snap > Snap 2D Transforms To Pixel`: default `false`. When enabled, CanvasItem nodes internally snap to full pixels, "useful for low-resolution pixel art games", at the cost of less smooth movement (especially with Camera2D smoothing). `Snap 2D Vertices To Pixel` works similarly, but **the docs recommend against enabling both**; "prefer only enabling" the transforms one. Both are only read when the project starts ([ProjectSettings](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_projectsettings.html)).
- For fullscreen, use **Exclusive Fullscreen**: regular Fullscreen is 1 pixel shorter, which can make integer scaling drop a step ([Multiple resolutions](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/rendering/multiple_resolutions.html)).

### 7.3 Unity (URP) 2D Pixel Perfect Camera

Sources: [Pixel Perfect Camera component reference](https://docs.unity3d.com/6000.2/Documentation/Manual/urp/2d-pixelperfect-ref.html), [Introduction](https://docs.unity3d.com/6000.2/Documentation/Manual/urp/2d-pixelperfect-intro.html), [Prepare your sprites](https://docs.unity3d.com/6000.2/Documentation/Manual/urp/2d-pixelperfect-prep-sprites.html) (Unity 6.2 manual, pages built 2026-02-05).

- Attach the component to the main Camera.
- **Sprite import**: use the **same Pixels Per Unit** for all sprites; `Filter Mode` = `Point`; `Compression` = `None`; set Pivot Unit Mode to Pixels.
- **Assets Pixels Per Unit**: match your sprites' PPU.
- **Reference Resolution**: the resolution your art was designed for, e.g. 320×180.
- **Crop Frame**: how black bars are added when the aspect ratio differs.
- **Grid Snapping**: `Upscale Render Texture` (renders to a temporary texture close to the reference resolution, then upscales it, giving unaliased, unrotated pixels) or `Pixel Snapping` (snaps Sprite Renderers to a world-space grid at render time to prevent sub-pixel movement, without changing Transforms).
- **Filter Mode** (only available with Stretch Fill): `Retro AA` (upscales by an integer multiple, then bilinear-filters the rest of the way) or `Point`. The docs warn that upscaling this way can cause bad pixel placement and lose pixel perfectness.

### 7.4 General Principles

Nearest-neighbor sampling (Nearest/Point), no compression, consistent PPU, integer scaling, and pixel-snapped camera and sprite positions. If characters need smooth movement, keep "logical coordinates" as floats and round only at render time. [the second sentence is common practice]

## Beginner Developer Checklist

- [ ] **Pick your base resolution first** (640×360 or 320×180 recommended), list your target screens, and confirm each is an integer multiple (§7.1 table).
- [ ] **Decide "one art pixel = how many screen pixels"** and never change it for the whole project; never scale individual sprites in the engine (to avoid mixels).
- [ ] Godot: `viewport` + `integer` + `Nearest` + only `Snap 2D Transforms To Pixel` enabled; Unity: Pixel Perfect Camera + `Point` + `Compression None` + consistent PPU.
- [ ] **Pick an existing palette** (e.g. Endesga 32 or Resurrect 64); don't make your own colors early in the project.
- [ ] **Decide character and tile sizes**: e.g. characters 16×24 or 32×32, tiles 16×16. Make 1 character and 1 set of ground tiles first, then test readability in-engine.
- [ ] Turn on Aseprite's Pixel Perfect when drawing lines; check that every diagonal has equal segment lengths and that curves change monotonically.
- [ ] **Set the light source first** (e.g. top-left) and keep it consistent across all sprites; avoid pillow shading and banding.
- [ ] Outlines: start with dark solid lines, and once comfortable try selout with region-varying colors; don't AA the outer edge.
- [ ] Use dithering only for transitions, and test for flicker while moving and scrolling.
- [ ] Do keyframes first: walk at 6 frames (low resolution) or 12 frames, run at 4 to 8 frames, start tuning around 80 to 160 ms per frame, and control "energy" through total duration.
- [ ] Use Godot terrain sets or a Tiled Mixed Set for terrain; validate with a 16-tile corner set first, then expand to a 47-tile blob.
- [ ] Don't rotate pixel sprites. If something must rotate, draw 8 directions, or use RotSprite and then touch it up by hand.
- [ ] Study 1 Saint11 or Slynyrd tutorial image per week by redrawing it; that works better than reading theory.
